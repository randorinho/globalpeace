## Why this matters

This is a working example of **Portuguese civil society inside the foreign-policy
process**: an association convening, at the Foreign Ministry itself, diplomats,
political decision-makers, academics and civil society around how the UN must
adapt. It is the institutional-dialogue counterpart to the
[APDSI Charter-reform letter](apdsi-un-charter-reform.md): the APDSI text makes
the radical constitutional argument; this brief carries the same core diagnosis
through official channels, tied to Portugal's Security Council candidacy.

## The diagnosis (summary)

- The post-1945 **rules-based system is eroding**: gross violations of
  **Article 2 of the UN Charter** (the collective-security core) and recurrent
  veto use — eight vetoes in 2024, a level last seen in 1986 — compromise the
  Security Council's central function.
- The **security agenda has expanded** far beyond armed conflict: food
  insecurity, global health, pandemics, climate change, migration and
  transnational organized crime act interdependently, above all in fragile
  contexts.
- **Emerging risks are insufficiently regulated**: artificial intelligence,
  cybersecurity and new military technologies — including hypersonic weapons
  *not covered by existing containment treaties*. The absence of effective
  regulation reinforces power asymmetries and adds strategic vulnerability —
  i.e., where legal boundaries are missing, political, economic and societal
  exposure keeps climbing.
- Against the "multilateralism is dead" narrative: most countries — notably
  small and medium states and the Global South — retain a strong interest in a
  system subordinated to international law, applicable to all. **Human
  security** regains centrality.

## Portugal's proposed posture (2027–28 UNSC candidacy)

Three structuring priorities — **Prevention, Partnership, Protection** — and a
selective thematic agenda: defense of international law and the UN Charter;
protection of human rights and vulnerable groups; the climate–security nexus;
food security and global health; ocean and maritime security; **governance of
emerging technologies (AI and cybersecurity)**; reform and effectiveness of
peace operations. Working methods: transparency, openness to non-members and
**regular briefings with civil-society organisations**.
